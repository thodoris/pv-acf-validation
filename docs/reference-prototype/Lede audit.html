Design audit · Opening paragraph

The opening paragraph appears in eight different costumes.

On every screen, the title is followed by a short italic subtitle and then — on most screens — a substantive opening paragraph. That second paragraph (the lede) is the element that has drifted. Eight visual variants are currently in production. This sheet inventories them, names what is inconsistent, and proposes three unified directions.

01b — Background surface

The lede sits on three different surfaces

A second axis of drift that compounds the first. Same conceptual paragraph, three different backgrounds depending on which screen you happen to be on.

#FBF8F1 · --page

Cream page

The default body surface. The lede inherits this when no panel is wrapped around it.

  • .lede · Submit, Thanks, Interview
  • .setup-header__intro · cluster setup
  • .tagline · every title screen
  • .grounding-closer · G2 close
  • .how-intro · Welcome
#F8F4EA · --surface-deep

Cream-deep panel

A hair darker than the page. The .lede-panel family paints itself in this colour, with a coloured left rule on top.

  • .lede-panel · C1 / C2 setup
  • .lede-panel--grounding · G1 / G2
  • .lede-panel--italic · closer callouts
  • .lede-panel--closer · G1 close

Note. The contrast against the page is only ~3 luminance steps — readable as "a panel" up close, but in screenshots and at distance it looks like the page is showing through unevenly.

#FFFFFF · --surface

White card

Every .q-card and .inst-panel is white. Anything lede-shaped inside one sits on white.

  • .q-card__descriptor · italic line under the question stem
  • .inst-panel__kicker + body · instrument-as-object panel

Defensible deviation. These are in-card scaffolding, not the screen's primary lede — they inherit the card's surface by definition, and should keep doing so.

3

Three surfaces, one element.

The cream-page vs. cream-deep choice is the visible split — the difference between them is small enough that it doesn't read as "a chosen container," it reads as a rendering bug or a stale CSS rule. White-card is a different conversation: it's the card's surface, not the lede's, and the lede inside is legitimately a different element (it supports a question stem, not the screen).

The simplest fix is to retire the cream-deep panel altogether. All three proposed directions (A, B, C) do this — none of them paint a background; the lede inherits the page. The cream-deep surface stays available for the right rail and other contexts where it earns its keep, but the lede stops competing with them.

02 — The drift

Why this reads as inconsistent

5
font sizes
3
container treatments
  • Five sizes for the same role. 13.5 px, 14.5 px, 15 px, 15.5 px, 16 px — none deliberate. Each one feels like the right pixel-perfect choice in isolation; together they look like a system that nobody owns.
  • The container changes meaning every time it appears. On C1/C2 setup the panel signals "this is the framing." On grounding it signals nothing (rule colour shifts, body identical). On submit it disappears entirely. Same paragraph role, three different visual claims.
  • Italic is doing two jobs at once. It signals (a) "this is the one-line headline subtitle" — the .tagline — and also (b) "this is a closing/contemplative paragraph" — .lede-panel--italic and .grounding-closer. The reader cannot decode which one is which.
  • The coral left rule is overloaded. It marks the lede panel and is also the active step indicator, the percent-time bar fill, and the relevance banner border. The lede has stopped being distinctive.
  • Closer paragraphs are a phantom system. Three different classes do the same closer-paragraph job (--italic, --closer, grounding-closer) and they don't look alike.
03 — Five directions

One element, five ways to settle it

Same opening paragraph, same closer underneath, treated five different ways — from "keep what we have, apply everywhere" through to a bolder editorial register. Pick a direction and it replaces eight classes with one (plus a closer modifier).

Direction A

Quiet typography

Conservative

Drop the container entirely. The lede is one larger roman paragraph, measured tighter than body. Trust the type.

Public administrations across Europe are increasingly using AI systems to support, route, prioritise, or partly automate decisions about entitlements, eligibility, service access, inspection, and contestation. The question is no longer whether such systems perform well, but how they become governable in public institutions.

This cluster does not ask you to evaluate the framework. It asks whether the problem the framework claims to address is the problem you have actually seen in practice.

  • FontIBM Plex Sans · 400
  • Size17 px / 1.7
  • Measure60 ch
  • Colour--ink · closer --ink-soft
  • Chromenone

Trade-off. Cleanest, lowest noise floor, ages well. Loses the "here is the framing" signal the coral panel currently provides — relies on placement (directly under the title) to do that work.

Direction B

Hairline rule

Refined

Keep the coral signal but strip it down: a single 1 px left rule, no background. Closer paragraphs reuse the form with a faint rule.

Public administrations across Europe are increasingly using AI systems to support, route, prioritise, or partly automate decisions about entitlements, eligibility, service access, inspection, and contestation. The question is no longer whether such systems perform well, but how they become governable in public institutions.

This cluster does not ask you to evaluate the framework. It asks whether the problem the framework claims to address is the problem you have actually seen in practice.

  • FontIBM Plex Sans · 400
  • Size16.5 px / 1.7
  • Measure60 ch
  • Rule1 px --coral · 24 px gutter
  • Closersame rule in --ink-faint, italic

Trade-off. Keeps a visual marker without the heavy panel. The closer becomes a recognisable second form of the same primitive, which solves the phantom-closer problem.

Direction C

Editorial drop

Bolder

Make the lede a typographic statement. Display family, larger size, short coral mark — reads like the opening of an essay.

Public administrations across Europe are increasingly using AI systems to support, route, prioritise, or partly automate decisions about entitlements, eligibility, service access, inspection, and contestation.

This cluster does not ask you to evaluate the framework. It asks whether the problem the framework claims to address is the problem you have actually seen in practice.

  • FontGFS Neohellenic · 400
  • Size22 px / 1.4
  • Measure32 ch
  • Mark32 × 2 px coral, 16 px above
  • Closer18 px italic, faint mark

Trade-off. Strongest editorial voice, matches the thesis register most directly. Costs vertical space and demands that lede copy be edited shorter — at 22 px, anything over four lines becomes a brick.

Direction D

Keep the panel — apply everywhere

Stay the course

Take the current .lede-panel from C1 / C2 setup and apply it as the single lede primitive on every screen. Retire the naked variants instead of the panelled ones.

Public administrations across Europe are increasingly using AI systems to support, route, prioritise, or partly automate decisions about entitlements, eligibility, service access, inspection, and contestation. The question is no longer whether such systems perform well, but how they become governable in public institutions.

This cluster does not ask you to evaluate the framework. It asks whether the problem the framework claims to address is the problem you have actually seen in practice.

  • FontIBM Plex Sans · 400
  • Size15.5 px / 1.65
  • Surface--surface-deep
  • Rule3 px --coral left
  • Closersame panel, italic, coral-deep

Trade-off. Zero net design change for the screens that already use the panel; the other screens come up to meet them. Costs nothing in vocabulary the system has already established. Risk: the cream-deep contrast against the page is faint, so the panel still doesn't read distinctly at first glance.

Direction E

Strengthen the panel

Heavier

Keep the panel concept but make it actually read as one. Deeper background, no left rule, larger padding, italic body. The lede becomes an unmistakable framing block.

Public administrations across Europe are increasingly using AI systems to support, route, prioritise, or partly automate decisions about entitlements, eligibility, service access, inspection, and contestation. The question is no longer whether such systems perform well, but how they become governable in public institutions.

This cluster does not ask you to evaluate the framework. It asks whether the problem the framework claims to address is the problem you have actually seen in practice.

  • FontIBM Plex Sans · 400
  • Size16 px / 1.7, italic
  • Surface--coral-tint
  • Rulenone — type+colour carry it
  • Closer--page-subtle, roman

Trade-off. The lede stops apologising for being there. Coral-tint is already used for relevance banners and active-step glow, so reusing it for the lede coheres with the chrome palette. Risk: stronger panels claim more visual weight per screen — fine for setup/grounding pages, may overpower question screens.

05 — Deviations that should stay

Two places where the lede should not apply

Question screens · in-card

Keep .q-card__descriptor separate

It sits inside the question card, supporting the question stem. Promoting it to the unified lede would put two ledes on the same screen — the cluster lede above and the question lede inside — which collapses the visual hierarchy the card depends on. Leave it as in-card italic scaffolding.

Welcome · "How this works"

Keep .how-intro as a section lead

Welcome has its own page-level lede already (the substantive tagline at the top). .how-intro introduces a downstream section, not the screen. Apply the unified lede at the top, and let this remain a section lead at body-text register.

Thank-you

May warrant a softer cadence

The final screen reads as resolution, not framing. Direction B's hairline can stay; Direction C's editorial mark may feel too declarative when the user has already submitted. Consider dropping to the closer modifier only on Thanks, with no primary lede above it.

Choosing between them

This is a genuine choice between five legitimate directions — not a foregone conclusion.

An earlier draft of this audit recommended Direction B. That was a designer's reflex toward "less chrome" rather than a system requirement — worth flagging so the choice isn't made by default.

The two axes that actually decide it.

  1. How loud should the lede be? A and B are quiet — they trust the position under the title to do the framing work. D and E are loud — they give the lede its own surface, so the reader cannot mistake it for body text. C is loudest in voice but smallest in chrome.
  2. Where does the change risk land? D is the safest — half the screens already render this way; the change is "extend, don't replace." A, B, C and E are larger interventions: every panelled screen has to be re-rendered and every naked screen gets visual weight it currently doesn't have.

Three honest recommendations, depending on appetite:

  • Lowest-risk: Direction D. Apply the existing panel everywhere; the system already speaks this dialect. The faint contrast against the page is a real concern, but solvable later with one token change.
  • Most distinctive: Direction E. Coral-tint reuses a colour already in chrome, the panel finally reads as a panel, italic body signals "framing voice." Best fit if the lede should claim screen space deliberately.
  • Most editorial: Direction C on G1 / G2 only, paired with B everywhere else. Treats grounding as a set-piece and the rest of the platform as workmanlike. This was the original recommendation and remains a reasonable composite — but it is the heaviest intervention.

Tell me which axis matters more and I'll wire it in. If "stay the course" is the right call, D is one CSS sweep away.
